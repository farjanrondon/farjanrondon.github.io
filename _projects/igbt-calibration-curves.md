## Goal

The on-state voltage only works as a thermometer once you know how it changes with temperature at each current. The goal was to measure that relationship on the IGBTs and antiparallel diodes of a three-phase inverter, and to build the calibration curves the estimation method needs.

## What I did

- Set up the inverter with its DC bus fed from two lab supplies and a 3 m cable between two phases as the inductive load, with pulses under 100 µs generated by the inverter’s ESP32 controller.
- Measured the gate-emitter voltage, the on-state voltage with [my probe](../on-state-voltage-probe/) and the collector current with a Rogowski coil, while heating the heatsink. A thermocouple next to the six IGBTs gave the reference temperature.
- Wrote a MATLAB script that reads every oscilloscope capture, takes the temperature from the file name, removes the probe’s offset, corrects its gain and builds the voltage–current curve for each temperature.
- Filtered the traces with a recursive low-pass filter run forward and then backward, which removes the noise without shifting the signal in time.
- Found that the 30 °C and 40 °C curves didn’t follow the trend. The hot plate couldn’t be set below 93 °C, so the heatsink heated up faster than the IGBT junctions. I replaced it with an electric stove controlled by a variable autotransformer, with a Pyrex tray in between to avoid leakage currents, and repeated the campaign.

## Result

The second campaign produced 17 curves from 30 °C to 110 °C in 5 °C steps, covering the IGBT and its antiparallel diode. In the IGBT’s conduction region the on-state voltage rises steadily with temperature at every current, by roughly 3 mV per °C at 20 A, which is what the estimation method relies on.
